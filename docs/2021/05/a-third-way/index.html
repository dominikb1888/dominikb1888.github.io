Stigmergy and the Plaza: Why You Cannot Mandate Digital Health

Posted on May 5, 2021

There is an old architectural parable concerning the construction of a university campus. When four modern towers were completed around a central square, the university rector was astonished to find the courtyard left as an unpaved expanse of lawn. When he demanded to know why the pathways had not been laid, the architect replied:

“The work is finished for now. In twelve months, let us look down from the top floor. The students will have walked across the grass, carving trample paths between the buildings. Where they have walked, we will lay our tarmac.”

The architect understood a principle that planners routinely forget: human utility cannot be deduced from a blueprint. It must be discovered through friction and use.

In biology, this self-organising mechanism is known as stigmergy—the process by which social organisms, from ants to termites, coordinate complex collective structures without central command. An ant leaves a chemical pheromone trace; other ants follow; the path deepens through use, or fades into irrelevance if abandoned.

Concrete highways to empty cities

Compare the architect’s wisdom with the state of healthcare digitalisation, particularly in continental Europe.

For two decades, health ministries and medical associations have attempted to construct national digital health infrastructure by bureaucratic decree. In Germany, the multi-billion-euro Telematikinfrastruktur (TI) has become a monument to the perils of top-down planning. Rather than observing where clinicians and patients naturally converge, planners have laid eight-lane concrete superhighways into the wilderness—requiring proprietary hardware connector boxes, complex smartcards, and punitive sanctions for non-compliant practices.

The result is a tragic farce. While ministries mandate certified portals that doctors loathe, actual clinical communication spills over into the unmonitored shadows: exhausted general practitioners photograph diagnostic findings on consumer smartphones and exchange patient updates over WhatsApp.

Data, like water, always follows the gradient of least resistance. When official systems are unusable, professionals do not stop communicating; they simply route around the obstacle.

The general practitioner’s dilemma

It is fashionable for digital advocates to dismiss physician resistance as Luddite obstinacy. This is both unfair and economically illiterate.

The modern general practitioner runs a small enterprise under immense operational strain. In Germany’s ambulatory sector, solo practitioners face an aging demographic whose multi-morbid conditions demand continuous care, while their reimbursement is governed by rigid fee-for-service caps. Expecting a rural family doctor to act as an enterprise IT integration manager—troubleshooting broken firmware updates while a waiting room full of patients coughs in the corridor—is a policy hallucination.

Worse, top-down digitalisation inverts the economics of medical practice. When a hospital or insurer buys software, it amortises the cost over thousands of transactions. When a solo practitioner is forced to buy proprietary hardware connector boxes, the cost is a direct deduction from their operating margin with zero operational upside.

Paving the trample paths

If state-directed mandates produce only friction and cynicism, how does one build a digital health system that functions?

By abandoning the fantasy of the grand design and learning to pave the trample paths:

  1. Observe actual usage before mandating standards. A digital tool is validated not when a regulatory committee approves its data protection concept, but when clinicians and patients voluntarily use it to resolve an operational pain point. Standards should codify successful emergent practices, not anticipate hypothetical ones.
  2. Align economic self-interest through contribution deductions. The current model subsidises hardware vendors while penalising clinicians. A far more radical, effective mechanism would empower the insured citizen directly. If a patient invests in validated digital prevention tools that demonstrably reduce systemic burden, deduct those costs directly from their statutory health insurance contributions.
  3. Treat data as a relational flow, not a static warehouse. Centralised health data repositories assume that health data is an asset to be collected and locked in a fortress. In reality, data is only valuable at the point of clinical interaction.

The future of healthcare will not be decided by ministerial decrees or ceremonial rollout deadlines. It will be decided by the humble dirt paths worn into the grass by exhausted professionals trying to care for their patients. The state’s only job is to watch where they walk, and bring the asphalt.
